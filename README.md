## Why Most Alumni Websites Quietly Fail

Let's start with the uncomfortable part. A lot of alumni programs struggle, and it usually isn't because people don't care. It's because the tools behind the scenes are working against them.

The common complaints sound familiar: shrinking budgets, small teams, outdated member data, and a website that was probably built once, in a rush, and never updated. Alumni relations departments end up spending more time chasing spreadsheets than actually engaging anyone. One analysis of alumni relations challenges puts it bluntly — reduced budgets, limited staff, and stale data are the top friction points, and they compound each other.

Then there's the design problem. Many alumni websites were built to look like a brochure, not to be used. There's no clear path to the directory, no easy way to register for an event, no obvious button to update a profile. Visitors land, get confused, and leave. The site becomes a digital ghost town, and the alumni office concludes that "alumni just aren't engaged" — when really, the website never gave them a reason to be.

The deeper issue, as some alumni-program critiques point out, is that too many programs start from "how do we get alumni to give?" instead of "how do we serve alumni?" A website built around the first question feels extractive. A website built around the second one earns the engagement that eventually leads to giving, mentorship, and participation.

So the real question isn't "should we have an alumni website?" It's "what should our alumni website actually do for the people visiting it?"

## What Good Alumni Websites Actually Need to Do

If you study the alumni websites that work — Princeton's, Stanford's, WashU's, CalTech's, and a handful of others — a clear pattern shows up. They're not built around the institution. They're built around what alumni come looking for.

Here's what that turns out to be, almost every time:

- **Find and connect with other alumni.** This is the number-one reason people visit. They want a searchable, filterable directory — by class year, major, industry, location. Stanford's alumni directory leans into this hard, with a backdrop of alumni headshots and direct links to update your profile or browse the network.
- **Discover events and reunions.** Alumni want to know what's coming up and register without emailing someone. A good site makes this a few clicks.
- **Give back — donations, mentoring, volunteering.** Vassar's site nails this with three clear pathways: Make a Gift, Volunteer, Be a Mentor. CalTech's donation page goes further, with recurring giving options and a matching-gift search tool.
- **Read alumni news and stories.** UGA's alumni news section keeps people coming back because it's actually updated. Princeton's homepage leads with notable-alumni stories, which builds pride and connection.
- **Access career resources and benefits.** Job boards, professional development, mentorship programs. This is especially important for younger alumni.

Notice what's missing from that list: a 12-section navigation menu, a PDF of the strategic plan, and a photo of the building. Those exist on a lot of alumni sites. They're not why anyone visits.

The takeaway for anyone building or rebuilding an alumni website is simple: design around user intent, not institutional org charts. Put the directory, events, giving, and career resources front and center. Make every important action reachable from the homepage in one or two clicks.

## The Platform Question: Build, Buy, or Subscribe

Once you know what your alumni website needs to do, you hit the next decision: what runs it? There are basically three paths, and they have very different cost and effort profiles.

**Path 1: Custom build.** Hire a developer, use WordPress with BuddyPress or BuddyBoss, or go fully bespoke. You get total control. You also get months of build time, ongoing maintenance, hosting bills, security headaches, and a site that breaks the moment your developer moves on. For a small alumni office with no technical staff, this is usually the most expensive option in disguise.

**Path 2: Alumni-specific SaaS.** Platforms like Hivebrite, Almabase, and EnterpriseAlumni are built specifically for alumni networks. They're strong on engagement features — mentorship, fundraising, events. They also tend to be priced for institutions with real budgets. Almabase, for example, starts around $4,990/year according to third-party pricing data, and Hivebrite is in a similar enterprise tier. For a large university advancement office, that can be money well spent. For a smaller association, a fraternity/sorority alumni chapter, a high school, or a professional group, it can be overkill.

**Path 3: Membership-and-directory platforms.** This is the middle ground. Platforms like Wild Apricot, MemberPlanet, Raklet, and Brilliant Directories are built for membership organizations of all kinds — associations, clubs, communities, directories — and they happen to map very well onto what alumni networks need: member management, a searchable directory, event registration, payment processing, email communication, and a website builder. They're more affordable, faster to launch, and flexible enough to handle most alumni use cases without a custom build.

The honest comparison across these paths usually comes down to three things: how much you can customize, how much you'll pay, and how fast you can launch. Alumni-specific platforms win on customization for the niche. Custom builds win on total control but lose on time and cost. Membership-and-directory platforms win on speed, price, and the fact that the hard parts — payments, email, hosting, security — are already done.

## Step-by-Step: Building an Alumni Website on a Directory Platform

If you're going the platform route — whether Brilliant Directories or a competitor — the actual process is more similar than different. Here's what it looks like in practice.

**Step 1: Define what your alumni actually want.** Before touching any software, figure out the top three or four reasons your alumni visit (or would visit) a site. Survey a sample if you can. Usually it's some mix of directory, events, giving, and career resources. Write these down — they become your homepage's primary navigation.

**Step 2: Pick a platform and start a free trial.** Most directory platforms offer a free trial or demo. Brilliant Directories gives you 7 days of full access with no credit card. Use the trial to poke around before committing.

**Step 3: Choose a theme and connect your domain.** Pick a template close to what you want, then point your domain (like `youralumni.org`) at it. SSL is included on most platforms, so you don't need to buy a certificate separately.

**Step 4: Set up your membership plans and fields.** Decide what tiers you offer — free alumni account, paid premium with extra features, donor tier, etc. Then configure the custom fields you'll collect: class year, major, current city, industry, mentorship availability, willingness to be contacted. These fields become your directory filters later.

**Step 5: Build the core pages.** Homepage with clear CTAs (Directory, Events, Give, Update Profile), an alumni directory page, an events page, a giving/donation page, a news or stories section, and a career resources page. Use the platform's AI tools to draft content if writing isn't your thing.

**Step 6: Import your existing alumni data.** Most platforms support CSV import. Clean your data first — deduplicate, standardize class-year formats, fix obvious email errors. Bad data in means a bad directory out.

**Step 7: Configure payments and email.** Connect a payment processor (PayPal, Stripe, Authorize.net, etc.) for dues, event fees, and donations. Set up your email sender and segment your lists by class year, region, and tier so you can target messages.

**Step 8: Test with a small group, then launch.** Invite 20–50 alumni to poke around before you announce it widely. Fix the things that confuse them. Then send the launch email.

**Step 9: Keep it alive.** The number-one reason alumni sites die is neglect. Schedule news posts, promote events, send a monthly email, and update featured alumni stories. A directory platform with email and content tools built in makes this much less painful than managing five separate tools.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid When Building an Alumni Website

A few traps show up often enough that they're worth naming.

**Mistake 1: Building for the institution, not the alumni.** If your homepage leads with "Message from the President" instead of "Find an Alum" or "Upcoming Events," you've built a brochure, not a community. Flip the priority.

**Mistake 2: Letting the directory go stale.** A directory with outdated profiles and broken emails is worse than no directory. Build in profile-update prompts — most platforms, Brilliant Directories included, let you trigger email reminders for members to refresh their info periodically.

**Mistake 3: No email strategy.** A site without a regular email cadence dies quietly. Even a monthly newsletter keeps the community aware the site exists. Use the segmented email tools to target by class year or region so messages feel relevant, not like mass blasts.

**Mistake 4: Overcomplicating membership tiers.** If you offer 12 membership levels with subtle feature differences, alumni will get decision paralysis. Two or three tiers — free, paid, premium — is usually enough.

**Mistake 5: Ignoring mobile.** More than half of web traffic is mobile now. If your alumni directory is hard to use on a phone, most of your alumni will never use it. Test on a phone, not just a laptop.

**Mistake 6: Launching and disappearing.** The single biggest killer of alumni websites is the launch-and-abandon pattern. Assign someone — even a volunteer — to own the site, post news, promote events, and keep the directory clean. A platform with built-in email and content tools makes this a few hours a week, not a full-time job.
